Guide

What is ERP software?

ERP stands for enterprise resource planning. In practice it means one system that connects the records a business already keeps, so the same information is not typed, checked and corrected in several places.

The problem ERP solves

Most growing businesses start with whatever works. Orders live in a register or a message thread. Stock lives in a spreadsheet. Invoices come from a billing tool. Payments are tracked in a notebook or an accounting package. Each tool works on its own, but none of them knows what the others know.

The cost is not dramatic, it is steady. Someone re-types an order into the billing tool. Someone checks the stock sheet by phone before confirming a delivery. At month end, someone spends days reconciling what was sold, what was received and what was paid. Mistakes creep in at every hand-off, and the owner never quite trusts a number without asking someone to verify it.

What an ERP does

An ERP keeps these records in one place and links them. A customer is recorded once and reused in every order. An order links to the delivery, the invoice and the payment. A purchase links to the receipt and the supplier’s bill. Stock changes only through recorded events, so any balance can be traced.

The main areas an ERP connects are:

  • Sales: quotations, orders, invoices and returns.
  • Purchasing: purchase orders, goods received and supplier bills.
  • Inventory: stock on hand, movements, transfers and counts.
  • Payments: receipts, payments and amounts outstanding.
  • Reports: pending work, stock and dues, drawn from the same records.

A simple example

A customer orders fifty cartons. In a connected system, the order picks up the customer’s details and agreed price automatically. It checks stock. When the cartons are dispatched, the stock reduces and the order records what was delivered. The invoice is raised from the order, and when the customer pays, the payment is matched to the invoice. At each point, the order shows where it stands, and nobody has re-typed anything.

What an ERP is not

An ERP is not a guarantee of accuracy. If the data going in is wrong, the reports coming out will be wrong. It does not replace good process; it makes a good process visible and a poor one obvious. And not every ERP covers every need. Specialist production planning, retail billing counters, payroll and statutory filing are separate areas that some products cover and others do not.

What to check before choosing one

  • Your workflow. Can the product follow the way you actually sell, buy and deliver?
  • Your exceptions. What happens with a short delivery, a wrong price or a return?
  • The systems you keep. If you use accounting software, how will the two work together?
  • Your reports. Can you see pending orders, stock and dues without building spreadsheets?
  • Growth. Can you add users, locations or controls without changing product?
  • Support and exit. Who helps you, and can you take your data with you?
