Guide

Sales, purchasing and inventory workflow

Selling, buying and stock look like three separate jobs, but they touch the same goods. This guide shows how the three connect, where records usually drift apart, and what to check in your own process.

Three flows, one set of goods

When you buy goods, stock rises and you owe a supplier. When you sell goods, stock falls and a customer owes you. Every order, receipt and payment is really a description of the same goods at a different moment. If the records for each live separately, they will not agree for long.

Order to cash

A sale moves through stages: an enquiry or quotation, a confirmed order, a check of stock, dispatch, an invoice, and finally a payment. The important point is that these stages do not always move together. An order can be delivered but not yet invoiced, or invoiced but not yet delivered, or fully delivered and only partly paid.

A workflow that squeezes all of this into one status hides the truth. A better one tracks fulfilment, invoicing and payment separately, so you can see at a glance which orders are waiting for delivery, which are waiting to be billed and which are waiting for money.

Purchase to payment

Buying has its own chain: a need, a supplier choice, an order, a receipt, a supplier bill and a payment. Four of these are events that people often treat as one: goods received, bill received, amount owed and payment made. Goods can arrive before the bill, and the bill can arrive before the goods. Recording them separately, and linking them, is what lets you spot a short delivery or a bill that does not match.

Inventory: every quantity needs a reason

A stock figure is only as trustworthy as the events behind it. Stock should change only when something recorded happens: opening stock is entered, goods are received, goods are dispatched, stock is transferred, a count is adjusted, or an item is returned. If a balance can be edited directly, nobody can later explain why it changed.

It also helps to separate the quantities. What is physically on hand is not the same as what is reserved for orders, what is in transit, or what is damaged and waiting for inspection.

Where records drift apart

  • Re-keying. The same order typed into two places, with two chances for a mistake.
  • Late updates. Stock adjusted at the end of the week, so every figure in between is wrong.
  • Edits without history. A number changed and nobody can say when or why.
  • Mismatched units. Bought by the carton, sold by the piece, counted in neither.
  • Missing links. A payment that cannot be traced to an invoice, or a receipt with no purchase order.

Returns and corrections

A return is more than a minus sign. The goods may go back into saleable stock, wait for inspection or be scrapped. The customer may be refunded, credited or sent a replacement. The invoice may need correcting. Treat a return as its own process, linked to the original sale, and correct mistakes through reversals and adjustments instead of erasing history.

A checklist for your own process

  • Can you see, for every order, whether it is delivered, invoiced and paid?
  • Do you record what arrived separately from what you ordered?
  • Can you trace any stock quantity back to the events behind it?
  • Do you know which customers are overdue and which supplier bills are due?
  • Do returns and corrections keep a history?
  • Does anyone have to type the same detail twice?

If the answer to the last question is yes, that is usually the best place to start.
